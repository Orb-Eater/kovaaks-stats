# Practice-Trial Duration and the Novelty-vs-Repetition Balance in FPS Aim Training: What the Primary Literature Actually Supports

## TL;DR
- **On trial length (Q1):** There is no motor-learning study that establishes an "optimal" single-bout duration, but the convergent evidence says KovaaK's 60-second default is reasonable and probably near the sweet spot for a continuous effortful task. The dominant risk within a 60s bout is not physical fatigue or vigilance collapse (both operate over tens of minutes) but the massed-practice performance depression seen in continuous tracking tasks — which argues for generous *rest between* bouts rather than shorter bouts. For reactive/click tasks, shorter is fine; for continuous tracking, 60s is well within the safe range.
- **On novelty vs repetition (Q2):** The evidence supports a "moderate variability" structure, not maximal variety. With 2,118 scenarios averaging ~10 runs each, the practitioner is almost certainly over-diversified relative to what the consolidation and specificity literatures support. A defensible structure is a small deeply-practiced core (roughly 5–10 scenarios worked to genuine stability and beyond) plus a rotating variable set of related variants, with the majority of session time on the core — especially because specificity-of-practice findings for aiming tasks are unusually strong and warn that transfer is narrow.
- **Biggest honest caveat:** Almost none of this comes from aim trainers. It is extrapolated from pursuit-rotor, timing, throwing, and lab tasks, and the single most relevant meta-analytic caution (Wulf & Shea 2002) is that principles from simple lab skills frequently *fail* to generalize to complex skills. Treat every number below as a prior to test against your own KovaaK's data, not a law.

## Key Findings

**Q1 — Trial/bout duration**
1. The massed-vs-distributed literature is about the *rest between trials*, not the length of the trial itself, and its clearest result is that massing depresses *performance* on continuous tasks — an effect that largely dissipates with rest and is partly a performance (not learning) phenomenon.
2. For continuous visuomotor tasks (pursuit rotor, tracking), distributed practice reliably beats massed; for discrete tasks the effect is weak or absent (Lee & Genovese 1988, 1989). Aim training contains both types, so the prescription differs by scenario type.
3. Within-bout physical fatigue of the aiming muscles is real but slow: wrist-extensor EMG fatigue in mouse-aiming studies becomes significant only after many minutes, and measured aiming *performance* did not drop across 30–60 minutes of work. A 60s bout is far below any fatigue threshold.
4. The vigilance/sustained-attention decrement is a tens-of-minutes phenomenon (classic tasks run 30–60 min; the standard PVT is 10 min), not a within-60s effect. Attentional decline is not a reason to shorten a 60s bout.
5. There is essentially no direct aim-trainer research on bout duration; esports fatigue studies measure declines over 2.5–3 hours, not seconds.

**Q2 — Novelty vs repetition**
6. Variability of practice helps, but "more is not better": the load follows an inverted-U, and moderate variability tuned to skill level is optimal (challenge-point framework, Guadagnoli & Lee 2004; recent variable-load studies).
7. Schema theory's variability prediction (Schmidt 1975) is empirically *equivocal in adults* — it holds better in children and for schema *formation* than for skilled adults refining an existing program (van Rossum 1990).
8. Specificity-of-practice findings are unusually strong precisely for *aiming* tasks (Proteau and colleagues): learned aiming skill is bound to the exact sensory/task conditions of practice, and mixed-condition practice can *hurt* transfer to a target condition. This is a direct warning against assuming wide scenario variety transfers.
9. Contextual interference (random > blocked at retention) is well-replicated for simple tasks but shrinks, disperses, or reverses for complex skills and applied settings (Brady 2004 meta-analysis; Wulf & Shea 2002; alpine-ski and bimanual studies). Recent meta-analyses (Czyż et al. 2024) find a medium benefit overall but with heavy caveats about applied/complex tasks.
10. Deliberate practice matters but explains a minority of performance variance (26% for games, r = .51, in Macnamara et al. 2014), and its core prescription is *effortful, targeted repetition on weaknesses*, not variety for its own sake.
11. Overlearning yields diminishing returns; consolidation and spacing across days do much of the work once a task is stabilized (Shea, Lai, Black & Park 2000).

## Details

### QUESTION 1 — Optimal duration of a single practice bout

**What "massed vs distributed" actually manipulates.** The classical literature almost never manipulates *trial length*; it manipulates the *inter-trial interval* (ITI). "Massed" = little/no rest relative to trial length; "distributed" = rest ≥ trial length. This matters for a KovaaK's user because the practical lever the research speaks to is how much you rest *between* 60s runs, not whether the run should be 45s or 90s. The Lee & Genovese (1988) meta-analysis (of a corpus of 116 retrieved distribution studies) concluded that when distribution is defined by ITI length, "massed practice depresses performance and learning (when learning is assessed by absolute retention measures)." Donovan & Radosevich's (1999, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 84[5]:795–805) broader meta-analysis of 63 studies with 112 effect sizes found spaced practice superior overall: "a meta-analysis of 63 studies with 112 effect sizes yielded an overall mean weighted effect size of 0.46, indicating that individuals in spaced practice conditions performed significantly higher than those in massed practice conditions." Crucially, they also found that "the overall complexity of the task being practiced was negatively related to the magnitude of the study level effect sizes (i.e., tasks high in overall complexity were associated with smaller effect sizes)" — a complexity–effect-size correlation of r = −0.25. Aiming is a complex task, so the spacing advantage is real but likely modest.

**The continuous-vs-discrete split (directly relevant because KovaaK's has both).** Lee & Genovese (1989) built discrete and continuous versions of one timing task and found distribution effects "quite different" between them: distributed practice strongly helped the *continuous* task's acquisition and retention, while the discrete task was far less sensitive to ITI. Translation:
- **Tracking scenarios** (continuous: smooth/reactive tracking — hold crosshair on a moving target) behave like the pursuit rotor. They benefit most from real rest between runs, and massing them will depress your *scores* more than it depresses your actual *learning*.
- **Clicking/flicking scenarios** (discrete: static/dynamic clicking, target switching) are relatively insensitive to how you space them; back-to-back runs cost you little.

**The pursuit rotor — the closest lab analog to tracking.** The pursuit rotor is a continuous visuomotor tracking task and is described in the literature as "perhaps the most reliable set of observations in experimental psychology" (Eysenck). Its century of trial-duration manipulation established: (a) massed practice produces a *within-session performance decrement* attributed historically to "reactive inhibition"; (b) after rest, performance jumps up ("reminiscence"), showing the decrement was largely a temporary performance effect masking intact learning; and (c) the length of the pre-rest work period matters — Eysenck, Iseler et al. (1969) showed post-rest downswing/upswing patterns depended on whether pre-rest practice ran longer than ~120s, and decrement appeared even after 20s of massed practice but accumulated with longer bouts. This is the single most direct piece of evidence on bout length for a tracking task: **decrement grows with continuous time-on-task and becomes meaningful somewhere past roughly 1–2 minutes of unbroken tracking.** Critically, warm-up decrement (Adams 1952) appears under both massed and distributed schedules, so it is not simply "inhibition."

**Within-bout fatigue — physical.** Two recent Brock University biomechanics studies (Forman and colleagues) fatigued participants with mouse work and measured both EMG and aiming performance:
- *Mouse clicking* (Forman, Melchiorre & Holmes, *Applied Ergonomics* 118:104284, 2024): 1 hour of clicking at 5 clicks/s. Aiming performance was largely unaffected (a transient accuracy dip after the first bout recovered by ~40 min), and perceived fatigue rose steadily. Their conclusion: "these findings indicate that mouse clicking may not result in performance fatigue, however, high levels of extensor activity may explain common injuries among gamers."
- *Mouse aiming in AimLab* (Forman et al., *Journal of Electromyography and Kinesiology* 82:102992, June 2025): six 5-minute bouts (30 min total). The wrist extensors showed significant EMG fatigue (mean/median power-frequency shifts), producing the highest muscle activity while aiming — "up to 9.3% MVC" during the aiming task, with extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) and extensor digitorum communis (EDC) reaching ~12.9% and ~11.4% MVC — yet **no aiming-performance metric changed across the 30 minutes**, attributed to "motor abundance" (shoulder/elbow synergists compensating for a fatigued wrist). Gamers hit 14.7% more targets and had 12.3% lower time-to-target than non-gamers, a stable baseline skill gap, not a fatigue-resistance difference.

**Bottom line: at 60 seconds you are nowhere near a physical-fatigue threshold that degrades performance;** physical fatigue is a multi-*ten-minute* accumulation and primarily a repetitive-strain/injury concern, not a per-bout learning concern.

**Within-bout fatigue — attentional/vigilance.** The vigilance decrement is studied over "30–60 min" sessions; the Psychomotor Vigilance Test (Dinges & Powell 1985) standard is 10 minutes (with 3- and 5-min variants). Reaction-time lapses accumulate over minutes-to-tens-of-minutes of monotonous monitoring. There is no evidence for a meaningful sustained-attention decrement inside a 60-second window of engaging, feedback-rich aiming — if anything, aim scenarios are the opposite of the under-stimulating vigils that produce the decrement. So **attentional decline does not argue for sub-60s bouts.**

**The three mechanisms, separated (as requested):**
- *Physical fatigue*: accumulates over ~10–30+ min; spares performance at 60s; matters for injury and long-session score drift.
- *Attentional/vigilance decline*: a tens-of-minutes phenomenon; irrelevant within a single 60s bout.
- *The learning signal / performance depression*: the pursuit-rotor "inhibition/reminiscence" pattern is the only mechanism that operates on the 1–2 minute timescale, and it depresses *performance more than learning*. This is why massed tracking feels worse than it actually harms learning — and why the fix is rest between runs, not shorter runs.

**Does the optimum depend on task type?** Yes. For **continuous tracking**, 60s is sensible and the main lever is inter-run rest; extending far past ~1–2 min of unbroken tracking invites performance-masking decrement. For **discrete clicking/flicking**, bout length is nearly irrelevant to learning; 60s is fine and you could mass these with little cost.

**Practical bottom line for Q1:** 60 seconds is a defensible, evidence-consistent default. There is no support for the idea that a single 60s bout is too long for either fatigue or attention reasons. If anything, the actionable finding is on the *rest side*: take real rest between tracking runs (distributed > massed for continuous tasks), while clicking runs can be packed more tightly. If you want to experiment, the informative manipulation is inter-run rest and total session length, not trial length.

### QUESTION 2 — Balancing new material against repetition

**Variability of practice: helpful, but inverted-U, not monotonic.** Multiple recent studies (e.g., variable-practice-load throwing studies, 2024) converge on an inverted-U: too little variability under-stimulates adaptation, too much overwhelms and can *damage* performance, and the optimum depends on the learner's skill and intrinsic variability. This is the challenge-point framework (Guadagnoli & Lee 2004): learning is maximized at an "optimal challenge point" where functional task difficulty is matched to skill level — high enough to generate interpretable information, not so high that performance (and the usable information) collapses. A 100-paper scoping review (2025) judged the framework "largely correct." For a wide-library player, the implication is stark: **2,118 scenarios at ~10 runs each is almost certainly past the optimal variability point** — the information from any given scenario rarely gets consolidated before you move on.

**Schema theory (Schmidt 1975): the adult evidence is weak.** The variability-of-practice hypothesis (variable > constant practice for transfer to novel variants) is the schema-theory prediction. Van Rossum's (1990) critical review of 63 studies / 73 experiments found the prediction supported far more consistently in *children* than in *adults*, where results were "equivocal, and in some cases contradictory." It applies best to schema *formation* (a genuinely novel task) rather than schema *attainment* (an experienced adult tuning parameters of an existing program). A serious adult aim-trainer is in the latter regime, where variable practice's edge is least established. Where a wider range *has* helped (e.g., Turnbull & Dickinson 1986), it was for transfer to *unpracticed* variants — relevant if your goal is generalizing to unknown in-game situations, less so if your goal is a specific benchmark.

**Specificity of practice: the strongest counterweight, and it is about aiming.** Proteau and colleagues' specificity-of-learning work (Proteau, Marteniuk, Girouard & Dugas 1987; Proteau 1992) is built substantially on *manual aiming* tasks — the closest lab analog to mouse aiming. Their repeated finding: with practice, performance becomes *increasingly bound* to the exact sensory conditions present (e.g., vision of the limb), such that removing or altering those conditions in transfer produces large accuracy losses that *grow* with amount of practice. Crucially, Proteau (2005, *Experimental Brain Research* 161:441–456) found that a *mixed* schedule of practice (alternating full-vision and target-only) **hindered** learning of a video-aiming task: "the results of the present study indicate that a mixed schedule of practice hindered learning of the task. Specifically, participants who practiced the task in the mixed schedule showed larger direction constant error in transfer than participants who practiced only in the target-only condition." This is direct evidence that, for aiming specifically, blindly mixing conditions can hurt. It squares with variable-practice recommendations only by scope: variable practice may aid transfer to *genuinely novel* variants, but specificity warns that skill at scenario A transfers to scenario B far less than intuition suggests — so a huge, thinly-practiced library buys less general aiming ability than it appears to.

**Contextual interference beyond the basics (as requested).** The user already knows Shea & Morgan (1979), Magill & Hall (1990), Pauwels et al. (2014). The key *additional* facts:
- Brady's (2004) meta-analysis found the CI effect is "typically smaller and more dispersed" in the field than in the lab.
- Wulf & Shea (2002, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 9:185–211) — the review the user flagged — argued that principles from simple skills often do *not* generalize to complex skills; for complex tasks, high CI and high variability can overload learners, and blocked practice is sometimes better early on.
- More recent complex-task work is genuinely mixed: Pauwels et al. (2014) found CI benefits for complex bimanual learning at retention (except for the hardest frequency ratio), while a Frontiers (2022) alpine-ski study found the CI effect did not cleanly appear in a naturalistic sport.
- The 2024 Czyż et al. meta-analyses found a medium overall benefit of high CI (retention: medium effect across 54 studies; transfer SMD ≈ 0.55 across 34 studies), but a competing 2023–2024 meta-analytic group (Ammar & Schöllhorn) found "insufficient evidence to support generalizable recommendations in favor of high CI, particularly in applied settings." **The honest summary: CI is well-established for simple lab tasks, contested for complex/applied skills, and aim training is a complex skill.**

**Deliberate practice (Ericsson) and the novice/expert axis.** Deliberate practice — effortful, goal-directed work on specific weaknesses with immediate feedback (Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993) — is the framework esports research imports (League of Legends, CS:GO studies). But Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald's (2014) meta-analysis found "deliberate practice explained 26% of the variance in performance for games (r = .51, p < .001), 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education, and less than 1% for professions"; a registered esports study (Collabra: Psychology 2024) even found near-null deliberate-practice effects at the individual level across CS:GO and League. The takeaway for the repetition/novelty balance: deliberate practice prescribes *targeted repetition on weaknesses*, which pushes toward a focused core, not a sprawling library. Skill level is a genuine moderator across the whole literature: **novices generally benefit from more blocked repetition (build the basic program first); more advanced performers can tolerate — and may need — more variability and interleaving to keep generating an information/challenge signal.** This is consistent with challenge-point logic (the same task is "too easy" for an expert and must be varied to stay informative).

**Consolidation, spacing, and diminishing returns on reps.** Overlearning studies show clear diminishing returns: once a task is stabilized, extra reps in the same session add little (physical-education and balance studies where 50%/100%/200% extra practice gave similar retention). Shea, Lai, Black & Park (2000, *Human Movement Science* 19:737–760) showed that spacing practice *across days* benefits motor learning (both a continuous balance task and a discrete timing task) more than massing within a day — consolidation (partly sleep-dependent) does work between sessions that further same-session reps cannot. There is even evidence (Savion-Lemieux & Penhune 2005; and a mirror-tracing study) that too many trials before a 24-h break can *reduce* offline gains — implying you don't need to grind a scenario to exhaustion in one sitting to consolidate it. **Practical reading: a scenario needs enough reps in a session to stabilize (roughly a dozen-plus quality reps, task-dependent), after which returns fall off and the marginal value shifts to (a) a new related task or (b) sleeping and returning tomorrow.**

**Esports/aim-training-specific evidence.** It is thin and mostly not about scenario selection. The honest state: aim-trainer transfer to actual FPS play has "limited peer-reviewed research," and most guidance is community extrapolation. Existing esports studies address focus of attention (an external focus improved AimLab aiming vs an internal focus; PLOS One 2023), training-vs-play-time surveys (PLOS One 2020), and session-length fatigue over hours — Sousa et al. (2020, *Frontiers in Psychology* 11:1030) found that after ~2.5 h of continuous ranked play, collegiate esports athletes were *faster but less accurate* (Stroop accuracy fell from 0.95 to 0.91, p = 0.042), which the authors framed as "a type of cognitive fatigue"; a 2024 pupillometry study found objective cognitive fatigue emerging over 3 hours of play before subjective fatigue was reported. None of these directly test "few scenarios deep vs many shallow." So the scenario-selection recommendation below is an extrapolation from general motor learning, explicitly flagged as such.

**Practical bottom line for Q2:** The evidence supports **a small, deeply-practiced core plus a rotating variable set, weighted toward the core** — the opposite of an evenly-spread 2,118-scenario library. A reasonable, testable structure: pick ~5–10 core scenarios (covering your genuine weaknesses and your key aim sub-skills — static click, dynamic click, smooth track, reactive track, target switch), and spend the majority (~70–80%) of practice time cycling through them across days until each stabilizes and then some; devote the remainder (~20–30%) to a rotating set of related novel variants for transfer and to keep the challenge point high. Introduce variability *within* a family of related skills (interleave several tracking scenarios) rather than across unrelated ones, and lean more blocked when a skill is new, more interleaved/variable as it matures.

## Recommendations

1. **Keep 60s as the default bout; make inter-run rest your real lever.** For continuous *tracking* scenarios, take deliberate rest between runs (distributed practice reliably beats massed for continuous tasks) — treat a depressed score during a long unbroken tracking grind as partly temporary performance depression, not lost learning. For discrete *clicking/flicking*, you can pack runs more tightly with little penalty. *Threshold to change:* if your own data show tracking scores within a session sagging after several back-to-back runs but rebounding after a break, that is the pursuit-rotor decrement — lengthen rest, don't shorten trials.

2. **Cut the effective library dramatically for improvement work.** Designate 5–10 core scenarios and commit the bulk of session time to them across multiple days. Your stat app should surface "scenarios with <20 lifetime runs" as *not yet consolidated* and treat the 77 scenarios above 60 runs as your genuine trained set. *Benchmark:* a scenario is "stabilized" when its rolling per-session average stops climbing and its variance tightens; that is the cue to reduce its share and rotate in a variant.

3. **Structure variability by skill family, and by skill level.** Interleave *related* variants (several reactive-tracking scenarios) rather than random unrelated ones; the CI/variability benefit is most defensible within a family and for transfer to novel variants. Lean blocked when learning a new mechanic, shift toward interleaved/variable once it is competent. *Threshold:* if a newly introduced hard scenario tanks performance for many sessions with no upward trend, it is past your challenge point — regress to an easier variant (challenge-point logic).

4. **Exploit across-day spacing and stop grinding to exhaustion.** Once a scenario stabilizes within a session, further same-day reps have diminishing returns; bank the gain and return the next day. Build your playlists to revisit core scenarios on a multi-day rotation rather than exhausting them in one sitting (Shea et al. 2000).

5. **Respect specificity for benchmark/transfer goals.** If your goal is a specific benchmark or in-game situation, practice *that* (or its closest analog) directly and heavily — aiming skill is unusually condition-bound (Proteau). Do not assume a wide library builds general aim that transfers; test transfer explicitly by checking whether gains on core scenarios move your benchmark or in-game stats.

6. **Instrument the uncertainty.** Because the literature is thin for aim training specifically, treat these as hypotheses your app can test on your own 22k-run dataset: (a) compare retention (next-day opening score) after massed vs spaced runs of the same tracking scenario; (b) compare improvement rate on scenarios in your deep core vs shallow library; (c) test whether interleaving related scenarios beats blocking them for your next-day scores. Your data can adjudicate what the general literature cannot.

## Caveats

- **Generalization risk is the headline caveat.** The strongest single meta-analytic statement relevant here is Wulf & Shea (2002): principles from simple laboratory skills frequently do *not* generalize to complex skill learning. Aiming is complex. Every effect size cited (distribution d ≈ 0.46, CI transfer SMD ≈ 0.55, etc.) comes from simpler tasks and should be treated as an upper bound that likely shrinks for aim training.
- **"Optimal bout duration" is not directly established anywhere.** No study identifies an ideal single-trial length for motor learning; the 60s recommendation is an inference from (a) fatigue/vigilance operating on much longer timescales and (b) pursuit-rotor decrement growing past ~1–2 min. It is a reasoned prior, not a measured optimum.
- **Distribution/CI findings are contested even within the motor domain.** Lee & Genovese (1988) found massing hurts learning; other reviews argue it hurts only performance. CI meta-analyses from different groups in 2024 disagree on how much high CI helps applied/complex skills. The learning-vs-performance distinction (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015, already known to the user) is exactly why acquisition-phase scores mislead here.
- **Schema/variability evidence is weakest in the population that matters to this user** (skilled adults), and specificity-of-practice findings actively caution against assuming broad transfer from a wide library.
- **Esports/aim-trainer primary research is sparse and indirect.** Session-fatigue studies (2.5–3 h) and focus-of-attention studies exist; controlled studies of scenario-selection strategy, playlist design, or few-vs-many practice do not, to current knowledge. The Q2 structural recommendation is an extrapolation.
- **Fatigue findings are about muscle and injury, not per-bout learning.** The Forman mouse-aiming EMG studies show real wrist-extensor fatigue over 30–60 min with preserved performance — relevant to RSI risk and long-session drift, not to whether a 60s bout is "too long."
- **Individual variation is large.** Challenge point, intrinsic motor variability, and skill level all moderate the optimum, so the specific numbers (5–10 core, 70–30 split, ~20-run stabilization) are starting points to tune against your own data, not established constants.
