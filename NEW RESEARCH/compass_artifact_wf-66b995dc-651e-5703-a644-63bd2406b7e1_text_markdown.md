# Should an Aim-Training App Show You Predictions About Your Own Future Performance?

## TL;DR
- **It depends almost entirely on framing and content, and the honest answer is that most of the relevant effects are small, contested, or drawn from populations unlike a skilled adult grinding KovaaK's.** Predictions that raise your expectancy of success and are framed as attainable and effort-based ("you're on track to hit X by date Y," "learners at your stage typically need N more sessions") tend to help learning modestly; predictions that lower expectancy, create a threshold to defend, or anchor a stopping point (a fixed projected ceiling, a "probability you reach rank X" that reads as low, a live PB-proximity meter) tend to hurt in-the-moment performance and can demotivate.
- **The single most directly relevant experimental finding is a warning:** the cleanest evidence on *showing a prediction* — education dashboards that display a predicted grade — is that it helps already-motivated users but *reduces* motivation for lower-motivation users (Valle et al., 2021), and pessimistic projected trajectories raise dropout (Conrad et al., 2010). Meanwhile the motor-learning "enhanced expectancies" literature that would justify showing optimistic predictions has largely failed to replicate once publication bias is corrected (McKay et al., 2023).
- **Practical bottom line:** show progress and trend framed positively and as "distance still to go" only when the user is committed; make all predictions clearly revisable and effort-framed; default to withholding discouraging, ability-framed, or threshold-proximity predictions; and let the user opt in to seeing forecasts rather than pushing them. Treat every prediction as an intervention on the user, not a neutral readout.

## Key Findings

1. **Self-fulfilling prophecy / expectancy effects are real but modest, and mostly studied as *others'* expectations, not self-expectations.** Merton (1948) coined the concept; Rosenthal & Jacobson's *Pygmalion in the Classroom* (1968) launched the field but was heavily criticized methodologically. The honest magnitude, per Jussim & Harber's (2005, *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 9(2):131–155) authoritative review, is that "35 years of empirical research on teacher expectations justifies the following conclusions: (a) Self-fulfilling prophecies in the classroom do occur, but these effects are typically small, they do not accumulate greatly across perceivers or over time, and they may be more likely to dissipate than accumulate." Jussim's own tally of teacher-expectancy effect sizes puts them in the range of 0 to .4, "with most falling between .10 and .20," and an overall mean between r = .07 (sample-size-weighted) and r = .17 (simple average) — with a striking correlation of –.71 between sample size and effect size, i.e., the biggest effects come from the smallest, least reliable studies. Larger effects appear mainly for stigmatized groups and when expectations are planted *before* the teacher knows the student (Raudenbush, 1984) — a condition that does not map onto a practitioner who already knows their own skill. The Golem effect (negative expectancies lowering performance) is documented (Babad, Inbar & Rosenthal, 1982) but far less studied. Critically, most of this literature is about one person's expectation of *another*; direct evidence that an externally supplied expectation about *yourself* changes *your own* performance is much thinner, and Jussim's conclusion is that people "are not rudderless ships" — they retain their own goals and resist others' false expectations.

2. **Goal-setting theory says a prediction is not automatically a goal, and whether goal-like framing helps depends on task type.** Locke & Latham's decades of work show specific, difficult goals beat "do your best" (goal-difficulty effect sizes d ≈ 0.42–0.82 in their meta-analyses), but only when the person is committed, has ability, gets feedback, and — crucially — the task is not too novel/complex. On novel or complex tasks where strategy must be discovered, a specific *performance* (outcome) goal can *harm* performance versus a *learning* goal (Seijts & Latham, 2005; Winters & Latham, 1996). Performance flattens or drops "only when the limits of ability were reached or when commitment... lapsed." For early-stage aim training (where the learner is still discovering technique and sensitivity/crosshair-placement strategy), a hard outcome prediction functioning as a de facto performance goal is more likely to backfire than the same information framed as a learning/process goal.

3. **The "enhanced expectancies" pillar of OPTIMAL theory — the most directly relevant literature — looked promising but has largely failed to replicate.** Wulf & Lewthwaite's (2016) OPTIMAL theory holds that manipulations raising a learner's expectancy of success (positive social-comparative feedback, framing a task as easier, feedback after good trials) improve motor learning and retention via a proposed dopaminergic/goal-action-coupling mechanism. The published literature showed a moderate benefit (g = .54; Bacelar, Parma, Murrah & Miller, 2022). However, McKay, Bacelar, Parma, Miller & Carter (2023, *International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, 18(1):242–262), re-analyzing that dataset with robust Bayesian meta-analysis and z-curve methods, found that once reporting bias is accounted for, enhanced expectancies and autonomy support together had "a small and non-significant effect on learning as indexed by performance at a delayed (≥24h)" retention test — concluding the true effect "is likely much smaller, if it exists at all." The primary studies had median samples of just n ≈ 14–18, requiring implausibly large effects (g > 0.8) to reach significance, and multiple large pre-registered replications found null results. Enhanced expectancies also appear to help under distraction but *hurt under stress* in at least one study. So the theory that would most directly justify showing optimistic predictions to a KovaaK's user currently rests on shaky empirical ground — an important honesty flag.

4. **Self-efficacy predicts performance mostly *between* people; *within* a person over time it can be null or negative — and this is the level that matters for an n=1 app.** The classic Bandura view is that self-efficacy causally boosts performance, and between-person meta-analyses support a positive relationship. But Vancouver et al. (2001, 2002) found that within-person, once past performance is controlled, higher self-efficacy on a given trial predicted *worse* subsequent performance (over-confidence → reduced resource allocation). Bandura & Locke (2003) rebutted that the tasks (a disjointed guessing game) were inappropriate. The Sitzmann & Yeo (2013, *Personnel Psychology*) meta-analysis largely vindicated the critics: within-person, self-efficacy has a weak-to-null (sometimes negative) relationship with subsequent performance, whereas performance strongly drives later self-efficacy. **For a personal app tracking one user across many sessions, this is the crucial finding:** a prediction that inflates the user's momentary confidence could, if anything, slightly *reduce* effort on the very next session.

5. **A stated target or forecast can act as a ceiling and an anchor.** Goal-gradient and progress-framing research (Koo & Fishbach, 2008, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 94:183–195) shows "to-go" framing motivates more when commitment is strong, while "to-date" framing motivates more when commitment is weak; a display proudly showing "80% done" can sap motivation versus "20% to go." Ceiling goals ("hit 100") invite coasting once reached. Anchoring (Tversky & Kahneman; Epley & Gilovich, 2006, for the self-generated-anchor mechanism) means an externally supplied number biases estimates and effort even when people try to ignore it. A projected-score number risks becoming both a stopping point (coast once hit) and an anchor that caps ambition.

6. **Knowing you're near a threshold can trigger choking in the moment — but this is separate from learning.** Baumeister (1984) and Beilock & Carr (2001) established that performance pressure raises self-consciousness and explicit monitoring of proceduralized skills, degrading well-learned automatic execution — exactly the kind of skill aim tracking/flicking becomes. A live "you're 2 points from your PB!" or "you're on the verge of the next rank" display manufactures precisely this pressure. Importantly, choking is an *acute performance* effect; it does not necessarily impair *learning*, and the two should be dissociated in the app's design.

7. **Constantly displayed, precise feedback can help acquisition but harm retention (the guidance hypothesis).** Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter (1984, *Psychological Bulletin*, 95:355–386) and Winstein & Schmidt (1990) showed that frequent knowledge-of-results improves performance *during practice* but can degrade *learning* (retention/transfer) because the learner becomes dependent on it and stops developing intrinsic error-detection. Reduced/faded feedback frequency improves retention. A prediction/metric that is always on-screen risks becoming a crutch that substitutes for the user's own developing feel for the movement. (Note: the related "self-controlled feedback" advantage from the same OPTIMAL tradition has itself been argued to be non-replicable — McKay, Yantha et al., 2022.)

8. **People are systematically over-optimistic about their own timelines (planning fallacy), so an accurate external forecast will often look *slower* than the user hoped.** Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 67(3):366–381) found 37 students predicted thesis completion in 33.9 days but took 55.5 days on average (~64% longer); only ~30% finished by their own predicted date. In their probability study, "13% of subjects finished by the time they had assigned a 50% probability level; 19% finished by the time assigned a 75% probability level; [and only] 45% finished by the time of their 99% probability level." The bias is specific to *one's own* tasks (outside observers over-estimate). This means an accurate, externally-computed prediction ("~103 more sessions") will frequently be *more pessimistic* than the user's self-generated optimistic guess — and the survey/dashboard evidence (Finding 10) shows pessimistic projected trajectories can discourage.

9. **Mindset framing matters more than mindset "level," and mindset intervention effects are small.** Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara (2018, *Psychological Science*) ran two meta-analyses: the correlational one (k = 273, N = 365,915) found the mindset–achievement relationship weak (r ≈ 0.10, ~1% of variance), and the intervention one (43 studies, N ≈ 57,155) found growth-mindset interventions produce d = 0.08 (95% CI [0.02, 0.14]), rising to about d ≈ 0.19 only for low-SES/academically at-risk students, with publication-bias signals throughout. The practical, defensible takeaway is not "install a growth mindset" but the *framing distinction*: effort-framed information ("you need N more sessions of practice") invites an incremental, controllable interpretation, whereas ability-framed information ("you are at level X / your ceiling is Y") invites a fixed, threat interpretation, especially after errors. This framing lever is cheap and low-risk even if the broader mindset literature is weak.

10. **Direct evidence on *displaying a forecast* is thin, and where it exists it is a caution.** In education, Valle et al. (2021, *Educational Technology Research and Development*, 69(3):1405–1431) ran an RCT (≈248 across two dashboard arms plus control) where a predictive dashboard showed each student's probability of a given letter grade: it *increased* motivation for initially high-motivation students but *reduced* it for initially low-motivation students, with a negative effect on extrinsic goal orientation (d ≈ −0.35, p < .05). Hellings & Haelermans (2020/2022, *Higher Education*, 83(1), N = 556) found a dashboard showing predicted pass chance and predicted grade changed online study behavior but produced *no* effect on final exam performance, with differential subgroup effects. Howell, Roberts & Mancini (2018, *Computers in Human Behavior*, N = 320) found the predicted grade level itself dominated students' affect and academic resilience (low predictions hurt both), regardless of message style or sender. Conrad, Couper, Tourangeau & Peytchev (2010, *Interacting with Computers*, 22(5):417–427) showed that when a progress indicator implied *slow* progress, abandonment rose and subjective experience worsened, versus faster-implied progress — with intermittent feedback minimizing the cost of discouraging feedback. Newby-Clark, Ross, Buehler, Koehler & Griffin (2000) add that people discount their *own* pessimistic scenarios but weight *externally supplied* ones more heavily — implying an external, accurate-but-distant forecast is more likely to be internalized (and potentially discouraging) than a self-generated one. And critically, **for fitness/quantified-self apps specifically there is essentially no experimental evidence on showing users a projected outcome** — the field predicts adherence but has not tested displaying those forecasts; the MyFitnessPal team (analyzing 1.4M users, ~79% AUC to predict goal achievement from 7 days) explicitly framed a "you seem unlikely to meet your goal" notification as untested future work.

## Details

### The core tension for your app
Your candidate predictions fall into two behaviorally distinct classes, and the literatures above cut differently across them:

- **Encouraging, effort-framed, revisable, opt-in trend information** ("projected PB given your current trend," "you're improving ~X%/week," "learners at your stage typically need N more sessions"): supported weakly-to-moderately by goal-setting (learning-goal framing), the *idea* behind enhanced expectancies (though see the replication caveat), and progress-framing research. Risk is low if framed as effort/process and attainable.
- **Discouraging, ability-framed, fixed, or threshold-proximity information** ("probability you reach rank X" reading as low; a hard projected-score ceiling; a live "2 points from PB" meter; "this scenario is past your challenge point" if read as "you've plateaued"): risks Golem/expectancy suppression, anchoring/ceiling coasting, choking under pressure, guidance-hypothesis dependence, and the demotivation-of-low-performers effect documented in the dashboard RCTs.

### Why the within-person point (Finding 4) is decisive for an n=1 tool
Almost all the "confidence helps" evidence is *between-person* (confident people perform better, largely because they're more skilled). Your app operates *within one person across time*, which is exactly the regime where Sitzmann & Yeo (2013) find self-efficacy's forward effect is weak-to-null and performance→efficacy is the strong path. So the app should treat performance as the thing that *builds* confidence, not the reverse — i.e., surface *achieved* gains rather than trying to pump up predicted confidence.

### The replication crisis caveat is load-bearing
Three of the literatures a designer would most want to lean on are exactly the ones that have weakened: enhanced expectancies (McKay et al., 2023), self-controlled feedback (McKay et al., 2022; now argued to be non-replicable), and growth-mindset interventions (Sisk et al., 2018). This does not mean the effects are zero; it means you should not build a strong product bet on any single one of them, and you should prefer design choices that are robust across theories (e.g., "don't manufacture pressure near a PB" is supported by choking research regardless of whether enhanced expectancies replicate).

### Where the evidence does *not* transfer cleanly
- Teacher-expectancy and mindset work is on children/students and academic achievement, not skilled adults doing a fast motor skill.
- Goal-setting effect sizes are largely from workplace/cognitive tasks.
- The choking and guidance-hypothesis work *is* on motor skills and transfers best, but often on novices and simple lab tasks.
- KovaaK's-specific research exists only as a reliability/measurement pilot (Jenny and colleagues; N = 10) establishing that its metrics are reliable — there is no published study on the psychological effect of showing predictions to aim trainers. Your app is, in effect, operating ahead of the evidence.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Default (show these, framed carefully):**
- **Show achieved trend and recent gains**, framed positively and as effort/process: "up ~4% over your last 20 sessions." This leans on the robust performance→confidence path (Sitzmann & Yeo, 2013), not the weak confidence→performance one.
- **When you show a "distance to goal," default to a "to-go" frame only for users who have demonstrated commitment** (e.g., a consistent recent streak), consistent with Koo & Fishbach (2008); for new or lapsing users, emphasize "to-date" progress instead.
- **Frame any time-to-goal as effort-based and explicitly revisable and probabilistic**: "at your current pace, roughly N more focused sessions — this updates as you improve," never "you are a level-X player." This is the cheap, low-risk framing lever from Finding 9.
- **Make forecasts opt-in / pull, not push.** The dashboard RCTs (Valle et al., 2021) show forecasts help the already-motivated and hurt the less-motivated; letting the user choose to look protects the vulnerable group and preserves autonomy.

**Stage 2 — Withhold or gate behind explicit opt-in:**
- **Suppress live threshold-proximity alerts during play** ("2 points from PB," "one rank away"). These manufacture exactly the pressure that Baumeister (1984) and Beilock & Carr (2001) show degrades proceduralized motor skills. Reveal threshold results *after* the session, not during.
- **Do not display a single fixed "projected score" as a prominent number.** It anchors and invites coasting/ceiling effects. If shown, present a *range* that updates, and pair it with "people frequently exceed their projection."
- **Be very cautious with low-probability benchmark predictions** ("~18% chance you reach rank X"). For a lower-performing or discouraged user this is the education-dashboard demotivation effect (Valle et al., 2021; Howell et al., 2018) and the pessimistic-trajectory dropout effect (Conrad et al., 2010) waiting to happen. If shown at all, convert to an effort frame ("here's the practice volume associated with reaching X").

**Stage 3 — Feedback hygiene (guidance hypothesis):**
- **Avoid always-on prediction/metric overlays during reps.** Fade the density of on-screen forecasting over time so the user builds intrinsic feel (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter, 1984; Winstein & Schmidt, 1990). Prefer end-of-session summaries to continuous prediction tickers.

**Benchmarks / thresholds that would change this advice:**
- If you can run your own A/B test (you have the telemetry), measure *retention-test* performance (performance in a scenario after a delay with predictions hidden), not just in-session score, to avoid the guidance-hypothesis trap. If a prediction display raises delayed-retention performance, keep it.
- If a forecast variant raises 30-day *adherence* without lowering delayed-retention performance, that's a win worth shipping.
- If any variant lowers session frequency for the bottom tercile of users, kill it — that's the Golem/demotivation signature seen in the dashboard studies.
- Segment by user: the evidence predicts benefits concentrate in already-committed/high-performing users and harms in low-motivation users. Personalize accordingly (e.g., withhold distant forecasts from users on a downswing or a lapse).

## Caveats
- **Effect sizes are mostly small and several key literatures are contested or failing replication** (enhanced expectancies, self-controlled feedback, growth mindset). Treat confident-sounding claims that "expectations boost performance" with skepticism — the strongest teacher-expectancy effects come disproportionately from the smallest studies (Jussim's r = –.71 sample-size/effect-size correlation).
- **The between-person vs. within-person distinction is frequently conflated in popular writing.** Most "confidence → success" evidence is between-person and does not license within-person, n=1 confidence-pumping.
- **Almost none of the primary evidence is on skilled adults doing fast FPS aim training.** The closest transfers (choking, guidance hypothesis) are on other motor skills, often in novices. The dashboard/forecast-display evidence is from education and web surveys.
- **There is no direct experimental evidence on displaying performance forecasts in fitness/quantified-self apps** — a genuine gap the field itself acknowledges (the MyFitnessPal authors flag their proposed forecast-notification as untested). Your app would be generating novel evidence; instrument it accordingly.
- **Individual differences are large.** Regulatory focus, commitment, baseline motivation, and skill level all moderate whether a given prediction helps or hurts. A one-size default will be wrong for a meaningful minority, which is the strongest argument for making forecasts opt-in, revisable, and effort-framed.
